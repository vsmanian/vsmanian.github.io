When the World Slept

October 2025

She was my சித்தி (Chitthi) - a word that describes "mother's sister" in a way that "aunt" never quite captures. She was a second maternal figure in my life, even during the times I didn't fully understand what that meant. She was also the first family member I got to know, not just as a relative, but as a person.

Some of my vivid memories from my otherwise socially reclusive childhood were the birthday cards I received from her. Every year, she sent me a birthday card (probably a habit inherited from her father, my grandfather). With beautiful illustrations or cool cartoon characters, those cards made me feel special in a way very few things ever did.

I cried on her shoulder when my mother went into surgery. She sat me down with my biology textbook and gave me "the talk" - something that was even more awkward as a repressed Indian teenager. She worried (endearingly) that I wouldn't pick a life partner by the ripe old age of 23 😅 She was a responsible aunt — like the handful of other wonderful aunts I'm blessed to have. But சித்தி was special to me.

The more I spoke to her as an adult, the more I saw her - I saw the quiet ambition buried under the weight of her duty and expectations; the spark in her eyes when she lived vicariously through the younger women in our family; the excitement in her voice when she spoke about something that inspired her. Even when she lived a conventional life, it never stopped her from saying "ஏய், போ பிள்ள!" once in a while, laughing as she did, whenever she decided to do something no one else approved of.

In a rare cross-generational bond, I found in her a kindred spirit. We exchanged movie recommendations, dissected Tamil song lyrics, and spent mornings at her kitchen table, the radio humming softly, புட்டு steaming on the stove, while she taught me to read short Malayalam phrases from the day's newspaper.

"பாப்பு, அது என்ன போட்ருக்குன்னு படிடா," she'd say, while sautéing something casually. Late at night, that same kitchen would see real conversations - conversations that waited for the world to sleep so that it was just for us.

She introduced me to the nail-biting world of வேள்பாரி, and I introduced her to the quaint charm of Anne of Green Gables. We both wept over When Breath Becomes Air when she received her cancer diagnosis.

She was my first phone call on Saturdays for years - me waiting to share all that happened that week, she eager to hear, for it wasn't real if it wasn't shared with her. She asked thoughtful questions, remembered every detail, and chronicled our lives like a proud parent framing their child's finger painting - nothing was too small to remember, nothing was unimportant to talk about.

Whenever I visited her in India since moving to the UK, we'd barely sleep the first night I spend at her home, for I'll get to know my family through her eyes. She had a way of shedding new light on people - that one the family thought was "odd" was just struggling; the one you thought was frail used to be the most eligible bachelor in town; the one whose opinion you took so seriously was just being a jerk. She was a gifted storyteller with a special appreciation for what time does to people - and she made sure we inherited her kindness and perspective.

Even when my relationship with her grew complicated, she always encouraged me to speak my mind - a luxury not many women of her generation were afforded. She was always the first to have that difficult conversation, the first to make peace, and the first to welcome people into the family with an undeniable warmth that my now-husband can attest to.

She wanted to know people and be there for them, even if they hadn't always been there for her. That's why people far down our family tree knew her beyond just her name. Whenever someone shared good news in the family WhatsApp group, you could count on her to be among the first to react with a heart emoji - her way of cheering you on.

She was smart, beautiful, and kind. She played the cards she was dealt and fought till the very end to be there for her loved ones. We continue to hear her voice in our minds, see her wisdom through the world, and will deeply, terribly miss her.

Mrs. Sivapriya Jayarajan passed away in Thrissur, Kerala on the morning of 05 October 2025 after an unyielding battle with cancer. She was 53 years old. Born on 02 April 1972 in Virudhunagar, Tamil Nadu, Sivapriya was the youngest of 3 children of TMNMSC Jeyachandran and Rajinimala. Known for her hard work, and curiosity, she graduated as a gold medallist from VVV College for Women, class of 1991 with a Bachelor of Science (Home Science) degree. A devoted wife, a supportive sister, and a fierce mother, she is survived by her loving husband of 32 years, VTR Jayarajan, her siblings Rajkumar and Sathya, and her two cherished children Ananth and Dr.Aswathy.
