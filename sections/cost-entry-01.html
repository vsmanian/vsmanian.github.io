Give In to Me

March 2026

12:43 am, the corner of my room with the record player. I am practicing a small riff from Michael Jackson's underrated (if there could be one) track 'Give In to Me' on my second-hand Electric Blue Korean Ibanez. I want to perfect it. Enough that it keeps me up at night.

After my two stints with maybe-the-right-instrument definitely-the-wrong-teacher, I felt like the joy had been sucked out of me. Piano is an instrument I find ethereal - I still get goosebumps thinking about the first time I listened to Black Lab's live version of 'This Night' or Dvorak's New World Symphony on the piano. There's excitement tinged with envy in me when I see people play something, anything at all, in the public pianos at King's Cross. It is an instrument hard to not sound beautiful in.

But the way you get introduced to music, I think, plays a big role in how it shapes your journey with it.

I remember seeing the Dangerous album for the first time at our unconventionally poor home. With parents who might be branded as reckless, we'd have no rice in the house but we had an audio cassette of Michael Jackson's album - an oddity for a small town South Indian household irrespective of socio-economic status. I was fascinated, even a bit scared, at the collage of images that make the audio sleeve. I listened to some of the tracks - and it crossed cultural boundaries even back then. Imagine the delight of watching the video of 'Black or White' years after listening to the song as a child and seeing an Indian Classical dancer!

My music taste hadn't developed enough to truly find 'Give In to Me' then. That came much later, when I discovered 'Sweet Child O' Mine' in college, leading me down a rabbit hole through Guns N' Roses, Slash, and his collaboration with MJ.

I wasn't going to go back to piano a third time. I was ready to make new mistakes. I wanted a whole new equation, not just to tweak variables.

I was offered a trial lesson with cello by the same school that offered piano. It was the closest to a divine experience I've personally had in my life - the sheer size of the instrument meant that you feel the music flow through your whole body. And I loved the teacher: she was witty, cool, and somehow managed to give me a second-hand experience of seeing colours in the music. It was perfect - but not what I needed right now.

I love classical music, but it couldn't hold all parts of what I wanted, not right away. I was too impatient for years of perfection and the "right way" - my shot at the "27 club" was already behind me. I was less driven to mimic the masters than I was to find my own voice. I needed a quick win, a way back into music, to engage the parts of my brain that logical thinking just wasn't going to do. I needed it to be fucking fun. I didn't need Yo-Yo Ma, I needed Mike Shinoda.

There's a theory that people have different personalities when they speak in different languages. As a bilingual, I know this firsthand - I almost have a third person perspective of the world in English, analytical and polished. I am more raw in my mother tongue, with emotions more on the surface. My hope is that learning music will help uncover a side of me that I don't have access to at the moment - like my uncontrollable urge to write that appears in the middle of a life event. I was starting to feel overwhelmed by this invisible need. And music, I hope, would help me be.

Music has always been of space and time. I feel the breeze through my hair when I listen to Michael Kiwanuka's 'Cold Little Heart', reminding me of my nomadic life as a pet sitter in a mansion in Steyning. Charles and Etta on my lap, watching 'Big Little Lies' with the title song showing the protagonists driving, with wind in their hair.

I see the lights on the Hyderabad road as I listen to Mazzy Star's 'Into Dust' - Jagadish and I driving around at night near the ISB road. Him smoking and my chest tightening with the enormous weight of a broken heart, begging to be rested on a friend's shoulder.

Whenever I watch 'Somewhere I Belong' by Linkin Park, I am immediately a teenager standing in front of our monitor that was also a TV playing the video on PS2, analysing whether this or 'Numb' spoke to me more. The summer I listened to 'Siragugal' by Yuvan Shankar Raja for the first time and the disappointment seeing the cut audio on screen. The laughter with friends when they saw how much I loved 'Nenu Nuvvantu' even when I butchered the pronunciation.

Music has also been about people, who just appeared at the right place at the right time, with new dimensions to explore. Some remain trusted shepherds, some inspire more. Some become memories, some become muses. My brother and his hand-me-down iPod Nano. The radio jockeys I grew up listening to, dissecting Tamil song lyrics. Glen's apartment. Jagadish's car. My aunt listening to "En Iniya Thanimaye" (My dear loneliness) with pure joy in her kitchen as she coped with her empty nest - the kind of joy that comes from being understood. Symrun's studio.

I walked into my guitar teacher's home studio with the resolute belief that this was temporary, my rebound instrument. It probably still is, one month in is too soon to tell - for I am as cynical as I am romantic when it comes to my afflictions. But walking into the warmly lit studio living room for music took me right back to the time I spent in Glen's apartment listening to 'Carnival of Rust' for the first time. It's a feeling I haven't had since moving to London 7 years ago. In trying to build my life intentionally, I did miss the serendipitous things - things that became a part of me when I wasn't looking.

Since starting these lessons, I am doing all the things I do when I want to do it well - obsess over every aspect of it, ask questions that either change my perspective or show me something that I want to internalise, have a singular focus on it until all else blurs for a while, until I need to come back up for air. I am also being trained to not let my analytical brain go on overdrive, but give space for a different, more tactile side of me to emerge. Sometimes you can't think your way through. Sometimes, you just have to play.

An artist recently told me about their five daily rituals that help them feel grounded, things that they could do today and be at peace if they died tomorrow. Maslow's pyramid for their soul.

I have now crafted my own list of rituals and that includes playing the guitar for a few minutes every day. It'll be good for my musical sensibilities and skill progression, but more importantly, there's something about being able to engage myself fully - eyes, ears, hands, and mind to this one pursuit without the 1000 tabs that are often open in my brain. The ability to quiet the world and let music take me somewhere I don't know yet.

For now, I practice the slide on the D string from the 1st to the 4th fret.
